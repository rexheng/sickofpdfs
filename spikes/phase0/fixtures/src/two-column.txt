The survey began in early spring, when the river was still high and the banks were soft. Each morning the team walked the same stretch of shore and marked the places where fresh sediment had settled overnight. They measured the depth of every deposit with a steel rule and wrote the numbers in a waterproof notebook. By the end of the first week the notebook held more than four hundred entries.

Most deposits were thin, less than a finger wide, but a few near the old mill reached the height of a boot. The team suspected that the mill race, though long abandoned, still shaped the current. A slow eddy formed behind the broken weir and dropped whatever the river carried. Fine silt gathered there first, then coarse sand, then a layer of leaves and twigs.

To test this idea they placed small wooden floats upstream and timed how long each one took to pass the weir. The floats that entered the eddy circled for several minutes before escaping. Those that stayed in the main channel passed in under thirty seconds. The difference was large enough to explain the thick deposits without any other cause.

In summer the river fell and the deposits dried into pale crusts that cracked in the sun. The team cut narrow trenches through the largest crusts to see the layers inside. Each trench showed a clear record of the spring floods, with dark bands of organic matter between lighter bands of sand. Counting the bands gave a rough calendar of the storms that had passed.

Not every trench agreed with the weather records kept at the town hall. Two of the largest storms left almost no trace near the mill, while a minor shower in May left a band as thick as any other. The team argued about this for days. In the end they decided that the height of the river mattered more than the amount of rain that fell on any single day.

The final report recommended that the weir be repaired rather than removed. A working weir would keep the eddy small and stop the banks from silting up further downstream. The town council accepted the advice and set aside money for the work. Repairs are planned for next autumn, once the river has dropped below the level of the old stones.
